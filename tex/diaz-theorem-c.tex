(written by Claude, an AI model, at the request of Carlo Perassi)}
\date{15 October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Diaz's conjecture says that a non-zero logarithm of an algebraic number never has
algebraic modulus. A hypothetical counterexample $u$, a \emph{candidate}, certifies
the three-dimensional space $H_{0}=\Qbar+\Qbar u+\Qbar\bar u$ inside $\Lt$, the
$\Qbar$-span of $1$ and the logarithms. Roy's strong six exponentials theorem then
excludes a number $z$ from $\Lt$ whenever the conjugation hull
$W_{z}=H_{0}+\Qbar z+\Qbar\bar z$ carries a $2\times3$ configuration. We decide this for
every polynomial expression $z$ in $u$ and $\bar u$.

\emph{Theorem C} (polynomials in $u$): $W_{P(u)}$ carries a configuration if and only if
$\deg P\in\{2,3\}$. The ``if'' half is one substitution in Diaz's Th\'eor\`eme~7(1) of
2007, which the project has formalised. What was not found in the sources read is the ``only if'' half: Roy's
theorem cannot exclude from $\Lt$, through its own hull, any polynomial in $u$ of
degree at least $4$. The proof normalises a configuration to Laurent polynomials. The
orders at $0$ and at $\infty$ then pinch the supports of the products into $[-1,1]$.

\emph{Theorem E} (polynomials in $u$ and $\bar u$) classifies the hulls that carry a
configuration into three classes. Class~(P) is Theorem~C. Class~(Q) is a family in the
exponents $\pm2,\pm3$, such as $\Re(u^{3})+cu^{2}$. Its configurations have a factor
whose involution exchanges $0$ and $\infty$, and they were not found in the project's
notes. Class~(R) is the project's real pencil spaces. In particular, when the
exponents stay within $\pm3$, a three-factor equation in the coefficients decides the
question. When some exponent has absolute value at least $4$, only class~(R) remains.
Real polynomial expressions in $\Re u$ and $\Im u$ with algebraic coefficients are
never excluded.

\emph{Theorem D} is a barrier. Adjoin to the logarithms a point $u$ of a circle of
algebraic radius that is generic over the field they generate. The enlarged system
still satisfies Hermite--Lindemann, Baker, Gel'fond--Schneider, six exponentials,
Roy's strong six exponentials and four exponentials in transcendence degree one, and
it is stable under conjugation. Yet it contains a candidate. So Diaz's conjecture does
not follow from these theorems used as properties of $(\cL,\Lt)$.

None of this proves Diaz's conjecture, and none of it is formalised.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}

Write $\Qbar$ for the algebraic numbers in $\C$, $\cL=\{\lambda\in\C:e^{\lambda}\in\Qbar\}$
for the logarithms of algebraic numbers, and $\Lt=\Qbar+\Qbar\cL$ for the $\Qbar$-vector
space spanned by $1$ and $\cL$. Both are stable under complex conjugation, since
$\overline{e^{\lambda}}=e^{\bar\lambda}$. A \emph{candidate} is a $u\neq0$ with
$|u|\in\Qbar$ and $e^{u}\in\Qbar$; Diaz's conjecture says there is none
\cite[\S5.1]{Dia04}. A candidate is transcendental (Hermite--Lindemann), and
\[
  u,\ \bar u\in\cL,\qquad \rho:=u\bar u=|u|^{2}\in\Qbar,\qquad \bar u=\rho/u .
\]

\begin{definition}
Let $K\subseteq\Omega$ be fields and $V\subseteq\Omega$ a $K$-vector space. A
\emph{$p\times q$ configuration over $K$ in $V$} is a pair of families $x_{1},\dots,x_{p}$
and $y_{1},\dots,y_{q}$ in $\Omega$, each linearly independent over $K$, with every
product $x_{i}y_{j}$ in $V$. Usually $\Omega=\C$; in Section~\ref{sec:tools},
$\Omega=\Qbar(T)$. Unqualified, ``configuration'' means a $2\times3$ configuration over
$\Qbar$.
\end{definition}

The only transcendence input of Sections~\ref{sec:statements}--\ref{sec:proofE} is the
following theorem. It is proved \cite{Roy92}, but the project carries it as a
hypothesis.

\begin{theorem}[Roy's strong six exponentials theorem {\cite[Cor.~2]{Roy92}}]\label{thm:roy}
There is no configuration in $\Lt$.
\end{theorem}

Fix $u\in\C\setminus\Qbar$ with $\rho=u\bar u\in\Qbar$; then $u$ is transcendental, and
$\rho>0$. Put
\[
  H_{0}=\Qbar+\Qbar u+\Qbar\bar u=\Qbar+\Qbar u+\Qbar u^{-1},
  \qquad
  W_{z}=H_{0}+\Qbar z+\Qbar\bar z\quad(z\in\C).
\]
We call $W_{z}$ the \emph{conjugation hull} of $z$. If $u$ is a candidate and
$z\in\Lt$, then $W_{z}\subseteq\Lt$, because $\Lt$ is a conjugation-stable $\Qbar$-space
containing $1,u,\bar u$. Hence:

\begin{quote}
\emph{If $W_{z}$ carries a configuration and $u$ is a candidate, then $z\notin\Lt$, and
$e^{\beta z}$ is transcendental for every $\beta\in\Qbar^{\times}$.}
\end{quote}

In this case we say that Roy's theorem \emph{excludes $z$ through its hull}. Theorem~B
of the project \cite[Thm.~10.6]{BP} decides this when $\dim W_{z}=4$: for any
$z\notin H_{0}$, $H_{0}+\Qbar z$ carries a configuration exactly when $z\in H_{0}+\Qbar w$
for some $w\in\{u^{2},\bar u^{2}\}\cup\{1/(u-a):a\in\Qbar^{\times}\}$. The project also
treats several families with $\dim W_{z}=5$. Its research notes are said to classify the
conjugation-stable five-dimensional case with a factor of degree two (see
Remark~\ref{rem:Qnew}). That classification is not in the repository and not
formalised.

\section{Statements}\label{sec:statements}

\subsection*{Polynomials in $u$}

\begin{theoremC}[Polynomial hulls]
Let $u\in\C\setminus\Qbar$ with $\rho=u\bar u\in\Qbar$, and let $P\in\Qbar[X]$ have degree
$k$. Then $W_{P(u)}$ carries a configuration if and only if $k\in\{2,3\}$. Explicitly,
write $P=p_{k}X^{k}+\dots+p_{0}$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item if $k=2$, the families $(1,u)$ and $(\bar u,1,u)$ form a configuration in
$W_{P(u)}$;
\item if $k=3$, put $c=p_{2}/p_{3}$ and $Q=u^{3}+cu^{2}$; the families $(1,u^{2})$ and
$(\bar u,\ u+c,\ \bar Q)$ form a configuration in $W_{P(u)}$;
\item if $k\le1$ or $k\ge4$, there is no configuration in $W_{P(u)}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theoremC}

\begin{corollary}[Quadratics and cubics are excluded]\label{cor:cubic}
Let $u$ be a candidate and $P\in\Qbar[X]$ with $\deg P\in\{2,3\}$. Then
$P(u),P(\bar u)\notin\Lt$; so $e^{\beta P(u)}$ and $e^{\beta P(\bar u)}$ are transcendental
for every $\beta\in\Qbar^{\times}$.
\end{corollary}

\emph{Credit.} Corollary~\ref{cor:cubic} and parts (i)--(ii) are printed. They are one
substitution in Diaz's Th\'eor\`eme~7(1) \cite{Dia07}, and already in \cite[Th.~2]{Dia04},
which contains it as the case $x=(v,\bar v)$. Th\'eor\`eme~7(1) says: if $(v,\bar v)$
and $(1,w,\bar w)$ are each free over $\Qbar$, then $v$, $vw$ and $v\bar w$ are not all in
$\Lt$. Take $v=u$ and $w=u^{2}+cu$. Then $v\bar w=\rho\bar u+\bar c\rho\in H_{0}$, so
$vw=Q\notin\Lt$; and $w=u+b$ gives the quadratics. The configuration of~(ii) is Diaz's
$(v,\bar v)\otimes(1,w,\bar w)$, with the first family multiplied by $u/\rho$ and the
second by $\rho/u$. The project has formalised the
theorem (\texttt{Diaz.Mirror}, \texttt{diaz\_2007\_th7}, part~1), and it also gives the
exclusion behind the project's Theorem~10.52 \cite{BP}, the case $c=\pm|u|$. The part
of Theorem~C not found in the sources read is~(iii), with its proof (Section~\ref{sec:proofC}). The case $P=X^{k}$ of~(iii)
is the project's power hull theorem \cite[Thm.~10.14]{BP}.

\begin{corollary}[A single polynomial tie of degree at least four]\label{cor:nogo}
Let $u\in\C\setminus\Qbar$ with $u\bar u\in\Qbar$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item For $P\in\Qbar[X]$ of degree at least $4$ there is no configuration in $W_{P(u)}$,
nor in $W_{P(u)}+\Qbar w_{1}+\dots+\Qbar w_{m}$ whenever $u,w_{1},\dots,w_{m}$ are
jointly algebraically independent over $\Qbar$.
\item For every polynomial $G$ with real \emph{algebraic} coefficients, the number
$z=G(\Re u,\Im u)$ has no configuration in its hull $W_{z}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

So at a candidate, Roy's theorem cannot exclude a single $z$ such as $u^{4}+u^{2}$,
$(\Re u)^{2}$, $\Re u\cdot\Im u$, $\Re(u^{2})$ or $(\Im u)^{3}$ through its own hull.
Several ties together can still be excluded. The project's Proposition~10.30 and
Corollary~10.32 \cite{BP} show that $u^{k}$ and $u^{l}$ are not both in $\Lt$ when
$4\le k<l$ and $l\in\{k+1,k+2,2k-1,2k,2k+1,3k\}$. For example, $u^{4},u^{5}\in\Lt$ would
give the configuration $(1,u)\otimes(u^{-5},u^{-1},1)$. In~(b) the coefficients must be
algebraic. Every real number is a constant polynomial, and $i/(u-s)+i/(2s)$, with
$s=|u|$, is real and excluded by Theorem~B.

\subsection*{Polynomials in $u$ and $\bar u$}

Put $s=|u|$, which is real algebraic, and $v=u/s$, so that $|v|=1$ and $\bar v=v^{-1}$.
Every $z\in\Qbar[u,\bar u]$ is $L(v)$ for exactly one Laurent polynomial
$L\in\Qbar[T,T^{-1}]$, and $\bar z=L^{\sigma}(v)$, where $L^{\sigma}(T)=\bar L(T^{-1})$. Let
$H=\spn(T^{-1},1,T)$, so that $H(v)=H_{0}$. Let $\pi$ delete the monomials $T^{-1},1,T$,
and put
\[
  M_{z}=\pi\bigl(\spn(L,L^{\sigma})\bigr),\qquad\text{so}\qquad
  W_{z}=(H\oplus M_{z})(v),\quad \sigma(M_{z})=M_{z},\quad \dim W_{z}=3+\dim M_{z}.
\]

\begin{theoremE}[Laurent hulls]
In this setting, $W_{z}$ carries a configuration if and only if $\dim M_{z}=2$ and $M_{z}$
belongs to one of the following classes.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\Alph*)}]
\item[(P)] $M_{z}\cap\spn(T^{2},T^{3})\neq0$; equivalently, $W_{z}=W_{P(u)}$ for some
$P\in\Qbar[X]$ of degree $2$ or $3$ (Theorem~C).
\item[(Q)] $M_{z}\subseteq\spn(T^{-3},T^{-2},T^{2},T^{3})$ and
$\dim\bigl(M_{z}\cap\spn(T^{-2},T^{2})\bigr)=1$. Equivalently,
\[
  M_{z}=\spn\bigl(T^{-2}+eT^{2},\ \beta T^{-3}+\zeta T^{2}+eT^{3}\bigr),\qquad
  |e|=|\beta|=1,\quad \zeta=-\beta e^{2}\bar\zeta .
\]
A configuration is $(1,\,T+\beta T^{-1})\otimes(1,\,T^{-1}+e\beta T,\,T^{-2}+\zeta T+eT^{2})$.
\item[(R)] $M_{z}=\pi\bigl(\spn(g,g^{\sigma})\bigr)$ with $g=G+\kappa T^{-1}G^{\sigma}$ for
some $G\in T\,\Qbar[T]$ of degree at least $2$ and some $|\kappa|=1$. Then $Tg=\kappa g^{\sigma}$,
and a configuration is $(1,T)\otimes(T^{-1},1,g)$. These are the project's real pencil
spaces \cite[Prop.~10.42, Thm.~10.43]{BP} whose data are Laurent polynomials. In terms of
$u$: $W_{z}$ contains some $w\notin\Qbar+\Qbar\bar u$ with $uw\in\Qbar\bar w$, and
$W_{z}\neq W_{u^{2}}$.
\end{enumerate}
The three classes are disjoint. When $\dim W_{z}=4$ there is never a configuration.
\end{theoremE}

\begin{corollary}[Explicit criteria]\label{cor:E}
In the setting of Theorem~E, suppose $\dim W_{z}=5$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item If $\pi L$ has an exponent of absolute value at least $4$, then $W_{z}$ carries a
configuration if and only if it is in class~(R).
\item Suppose $z\equiv A\bar u^{3}+B\bar u^{2}+Cu^{2}+Du^{3}\pmod{H_{0}}$. Put
$N=|A|^{2}-|D|^{2}$ and $\Delta=A\bar C-B\bar D$. Then $W_{z}$ is in class
(P) iff $\Delta=0$; in class (Q) iff $N=0$ and $(A,D)\neq(0,0)$; and in class (R) iff
$|\Delta|=|u|\,|N|\neq0$. So $W_{z}$ carries a configuration if and only if
\[
  N\cdot\Delta\cdot\bigl(|\Delta|^{2}-|u|^{2}N^{2}\bigr)=0 .
\]
\end{enumerate}
At a candidate, every $z$ in classes (P), (Q) or (R) lies outside $\Lt$. Examples are
$\Re(u^{3})+cu^{2}$ for $c\in\Qbar^{\times}$, which is in class (Q) with $A=D=\tfrac12$ and
$B=0$, $C=c$; and $(u/s)^{3}+(\bar u/s)^{4}$, which is in class (R). By contrast,
$\Re(u^{3})$ alone (the case $c=0$) is real, and by Corollary~\ref{cor:nogo}(b) it
cannot be excluded.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Class (Q) and the project's research notes]\label{rem:Qnew}
In class (Q) the factor $(1,T+\beta T^{-1})$ is a ratio of degree two. Its involution
$T\mapsto\beta/T$ commutes with $\sigma$, since $|\beta|=1$, and exchanges $0$ and $\infty$.
The project's research notes classify the conjugation-stable invisible spaces with a factor of
degree two into three families, for every such involution; the companion note \cite{CN} and the
README give a formal member of each, all for $T\mapsto-T$. Class (Q) is the cyclic family for
$T\mapsto\beta/T$ on its orbit $\{0,\infty\}$: with $h=T+\beta T^{-1}$,
$\varphi_{0}=\beta^{-1}T^{-1}+eT$ and $\varphi_{1}=\zeta T$, one has
$T^{-2}+eT^{2}\equiv\varphi_{0}h$ and $\beta T^{-3}+\zeta T^{2}+eT^{3}\equiv\varphi_{0}h^{2}+\varphi_{1}h$
modulo $H$. The notes only sketch the local cases at $0$ and $\infty$, so for Laurent data
Theorem~E settles a case they leave open. A (Q) space is conjugation-stable and invisible to
Theorem~B. For
$z=\Re u\,\Im u+i(\Re u)^{2}\Im u$, exact computation gives
$\dim(W_{z}\cap T^{-2}W_{z})=1$ and $\dim(W_{z}\cap T^{-1}W_{z})=2$. So that hull has no
factor $(1,u^{2})$ and no factor of degree one. As exclusions at a candidate, (Q) and (R) are
substitutions in Roy's theorem (for (Q) also in \cite[Th\'eor\`eme~4(1)]{Dia07} with
$x=(1,(u+\beta\bar u)/s)$). What was not found in the sources read is the ``only if''
direction of Theorem~E.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{A barrier}

\begin{theoremD}[A generic candidate satisfies (D2)--(D4)]
Let $F$ be the subfield of $\C$ generated by $\Qbar$ and $\cL$; it is countable and stable
under conjugation. Let $\rho>0$ be real algebraic, and let $u$ be a point of the circle
$|u|^{2}=\rho$ that is transcendental over $F$. Such points exist, since the circle is
uncountable and the algebraic closure of $F$ is countable. Put
\[
  \cL_{u}=\cL+\Q u+\Q\bar u,\qquad \Lt_{u}=\Qbar+\Qbar\cL_{u}=\Lt+\Qbar u+\Qbar\bar u .
\]
Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(D\arabic*)}]
\item $\cL_{u}\supseteq\cL$ is a $\Q$-vector space stable under conjugation. It contains
$u$ and $\bar u$, with $u\notin\Qbar$ and $u\bar u=\rho\in\Qbar$; so $u$ is a candidate for
$\cL_{u}$.
\item \emph{(Baker)} If $\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\cL_{u}$ are linearly independent
over $\Q$, then $1,\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}$ are linearly independent over $\Qbar$.
In particular $\cL_{u}\cap\Qbar=\{0\}$ (Hermite--Lindemann). Also, if
$\lambda,\beta\lambda\in\cL_{u}$ with $\lambda\neq0$ and $\beta\in\Qbar$, then $\beta\in\Q$
(Gel'fond--Schneider).
\item \emph{(Roy)} There is no configuration in $\Lt_{u}$. \emph{(Six exponentials)} There
is no $2\times3$ configuration over $\Q$ in $\cL_{u}$.
\item \emph{(Four exponentials in transcendence degree one)} Let $N$ be a singular
$2\times2$ matrix whose entries are non-zero, lie in $\cL_{u}$, and generate a field of
transcendence degree at most one over $\Q$. Then the rows of $N$, or its columns, are
linearly dependent over $\Q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theoremD}

So the system $(\cL_{u},\Lt_{u})$ is conjugation-stable, satisfies (D2)--(D4), and
contains a candidate. Hence \emph{no derivation that uses only (D2)--(D4) and
conjugation, as axioms about $(\cL,\Lt)$, can prove Diaz's conjecture.} These theorems
are the main transcendence inputs of the project's unconditional results about
candidates and of Diaz's papers \cite{Dia04,Dia07}. Theorem~D does not cover statements
about the values $e^{x_{i}y_{j}}$ themselves, such as Waldschmidt's theorem of 1973. Nor
does it cover the rank bounds for larger matrices (Remark~\ref{rem:scopeD}). The strong
four exponentials conjecture fails in $(\cL_{u},\Lt_{u})$, as it must: the certificate
$(1,u)\otimes(1,\bar u)$ is a $2\times2$ configuration in $\Lt_{u}$.
\section{Tools}\label{sec:tools}

Since $u$ is transcendental, $f\mapsto f(u)$ is an injective homomorphism from
$\Qbar(T)$ into $\C$. Conjugation corresponds to the semilinear involution
\[
  f\longmapsto f^{\sigma},\qquad f^{\sigma}(T)=\bar f(\rho/T),
\]
where $\bar f$ has conjugated coefficients. Indeed $\overline{f(u)}=\bar f(\bar u)=\bar f(\rho/u)$.
We use the valuations $\ord_{0}$ and $\ord_{\infty}=-\deg$ of $\Qbar(T)$. For a Laurent
polynomial $f=\sum_{j}f_{j}T^{j}\neq0$, $\ord_{0}f$ is its smallest exponent and $\deg f$ its
largest, and its \emph{support} is the interval $[\ord_{0}f,\deg f]$.

\begin{lemma}[Orders of a subspace]\label{lem:orders}
Let $\nu$ be $\ord_{0}$ or $\ord_{\infty}$, and let $V\subseteq\Qbar(T)$ be a $\Qbar$-subspace
of finite dimension. Then $\nu(V\setminus\{0\})$ has exactly $\dim V$ elements.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The residue field of $\nu$ is $\Qbar$. If $f,g\in V$ have the same value $n$, some
$c\in\Qbar^{\times}$ makes $\nu(f-cg)>n$. So each quotient $V_{\ge n}/V_{>n}$ of the
filtration by values has dimension at most one, and it is non-zero exactly when $n$
is a value. This is the valuation argument of \cite[\S7, proof of Th.~33]{BSZ17} that the project uses for
its Lemma~10.17 in $\Qbar[T]$.
\end{proof}

For a configuration $x\otimes y$ write $\mathbf X=\spn(x_{1},x_{2})$ and
$\mathbf Y=\spn(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})$. For $f\in\mathbf X$ and $g\in\mathbf Y$ the product
$fg$ is a combination of the $x_{i}y_{j}$, so it lies in $V$. With
Lemma~\ref{lem:orders} this gives:

\begin{lemma}[Sumsets]\label{lem:sumset}
Let $x\otimes y$ be a configuration over $\Qbar$ in a $\Qbar$-space $V\subseteq\Qbar(T)$,
with $x_{i},y_{j}\in\Qbar(T)$, and let $\nu=\ord_{0}$ or $\ord_{\infty}$. Then $A=\nu(\mathbf X\setminus0)$ and
$B=\nu(\mathbf Y\setminus0)$ have $2$ and $3$ elements, and $A+B\subseteq\nu(V\setminus0)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Normalisation]\label{lem:normal}
Let $V\subseteq\Qbar[T,T^{-1}]$ and let $x\otimes y$ be a configuration in
$V(u)=\{f(u):f\in V\}$. Then there is a configuration $x'\otimes y'$ with the same six
products, such that $x'_{i}=f_{i}(u)$ and $y'_{j}=g_{j}(u)$ for Laurent polynomials
$f_{i},g_{j}\in\Qbar[T,T^{-1}]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Products of non-zero numbers are non-zero, so $x_{1}y_{1}\neq0$. The numbers
$x_{2}/x_{1}=x_{2}y_{1}/(x_{1}y_{1})$ and $x_{1}y_{j}$ lie in $\Qbar(u)$. Replacing $x_{i}$
by $x_{i}/x_{1}$ and $y_{j}$ by $x_{1}y_{j}$ changes neither the products nor the
independence. So we may assume $x_{i}=f_{i}(u)$ and $y_{j}=g_{j}(u)$ with
$f_{i},g_{j}\in\Qbar(T)$, and $f_{i}g_{j}\in V$. Every place of $\Qbar(T)$ other than
$0,\infty$ is $T=p$ with $p\in\Qbar^{\times}$. Let
$m_{p}=\min(\ord_{p}f_{1},\ord_{p}f_{2})$, which is $0$ for all but finitely many $p$, and
let $\phi=\prod_{p}(T-p)^{-m_{p}}$. Then $\phi f_{1},\phi f_{2}$ have no pole on
$\Qbar^{\times}$. At each $p$ one of them, say $\phi f_{i}$, has order $0$. Since
$(\phi f_{i})(\phi^{-1}g_{j})=f_{i}g_{j}\in V$ has no pole at $p$, neither has
$\phi^{-1}g_{j}$. Rational functions without poles on $\Qbar^{\times}$ are Laurent
polynomials. Replace $f_{i},g_{j}$ by $\phi f_{i},\phi^{-1}g_{j}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A rigid five-element set]\label{lem:combinatorics}
Let $k\ge4$ and $2\le m\le k$, and let $S=\{-k,-1,0,1,m\}$. If $A+B\subseteq S$ with
$|A|=2$ and $|B|=3$, then $m=2$ and $A+B=\{-1,0,1,2\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $A=\{a,a+d\}$ with $d\ge1$. The three numbers $a+b$, $b\in B$, satisfy $s\in S$
and $s+d\in S$, so the difference $d$ occurs at least three times among pairs of
elements of $S$. The ten positive differences of $S$ are
\[
  1,\ 1,\ 2,\ m-1,\ m,\ m+1\qquad\text{and}\qquad k-1,\ k,\ k+1,\ k+m,
\]
split into three groups. In $G=\{k-1,k,k+1,k+m\}$ the values are distinct and at least
$3$. In $\{m-1,m,m+1\}$ they are distinct. The rest is $\{1,1,2\}$. A value
$d\ge3$ occurs at most once in each of the first two groups and not in the third, so
at most twice. The value $2$ occurs once in the third group, at most once in the second
and never in $G$. The value $1$ occurs twice in the third group, never in $G$, and in
the second group only as $m-1=1$. So a difference occurs three times only if $d=1$ and
$m=2$. The three pairs are then $(-1,0),(0,1),(1,2)$, so $a+B=\{-1,0,1\}$ and
$A+B=\{-1,0,1,2\}$.
\end{proof}

The lemma was also checked by machine for every $4\le k<80$ and $2\le m\le k$.

\section{Proof of Theorem C}\label{sec:proofC}

Write $P(u)\equiv p_{2}u^{2}+p_{3}u^{3}+\dots+p_{k}u^{k}\pmod{H_{0}}$. Conjugation fixes
$H_{0}$, so $W_{P(u)}$ is spanned by $H_{0}$, by $P(u)$ and by its conjugate.

\emph{Degree at most one.} Then $W_{P(u)}=H_{0}=\spn(1,T,T^{-1})(u)$ has dimension $3$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:normal} a configuration could be taken in $\Qbar[T,T^{-1}]$. Then
Lemma~\ref{lem:sumset} gives at least $|A+B|\ge|A|+|B|-1=4$ orders at $0$ inside a
three-dimensional space, which is impossible.

\emph{Degree two.} Here $u^{2}\in W_{P(u)}$, because $P(u)-p_{2}u^{2}\in H_{0}$. The
products of $(1,u)$ and $(\bar u,1,u)$ are $\bar u,1,u,\rho,u,u^{2}$. Both families are free
because $u$ is transcendental and $\bar u=\rho/u$.

\emph{Degree three.} Here $Q=u^{3}+cu^{2}\equiv P(u)/p_{3}$ modulo $H_{0}$, so $Q$ and
$\bar Q$ lie in $W_{P(u)}$. The six products of $(1,u^{2})$ and $(\bar u,u+c,\bar Q)$ are
\[
  \bar u,\qquad u+c,\qquad \bar Q,\qquad u^{2}\bar u=\rho u,\qquad u^{2}(u+c)=Q,\qquad
  u^{2}\bar Q=u^{2}\bar u^{3}+\bar c\,u^{2}\bar u^{2}=\rho^{2}\bar u+\bar c\rho^{2},
\]
all in $W_{P(u)}$. The family $(1,u^{2})$ is free. So is the family
$(\bar u,u+c,\bar Q)$, the value at $u$ of $(\rho T^{-1},\,T+c,\,\rho^{3}T^{-3}+\bar c\rho^{2}T^{-2})$.
These Laurent polynomials have distinct orders at $T=0$: $-1$; then $0$, or $1$ if $c=0$;
then $-3$. So they are independent, and evaluation at $u$ is injective.

\emph{Degree at least four.} Let $\mathcal W\subseteq\Qbar[T,T^{-1}]$ be spanned by
\[
  1,\quad T,\quad T^{-1},\quad L=\sum_{j=2}^{k}p_{j}T^{j},\quad
  L^{\sigma}=\sum_{j=2}^{k}\bar p_{j}\rho^{j}T^{-j}.
\]
Then $\mathcal W(u)=W_{P(u)}$. Let $m=\min\{j\ge2:p_{j}\neq0\}$. An element
$h+\alpha L+\beta L^{\sigma}$ with $h\in\spn(1,T,T^{-1})$ has order $-k$ at $0$ if
$\beta\neq0$. Otherwise its order is $\min(\ord_{0}h,m)$, or $\ord_{0}h$ when
$\alpha=0$. Hence
\[
  \ord_{0}(\mathcal W\setminus0)=\{-k,-1,0,1,m\},\qquad
  \deg(\mathcal W\setminus0)=\{-m,-1,0,1,k\}.
\]
The second set follows from the first by the symmetry $f\mapsto f^{\sigma}$, which
exchanges $\ord_{0}$ and $-\deg$.

Suppose $x\otimes y$ is a configuration in $W_{P(u)}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:normal} we may
take $\mathbf X,\mathbf Y\subseteq\Qbar[T,T^{-1}]$ with $\mathbf X\mathbf Y\subseteq\mathcal W$.
Let $A_{0},B_{0}$ be the sets of orders at $0$ of $\mathbf X$ and $\mathbf Y$, and $D_{A},D_{B}$
their sets of degrees. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:sumset} and~\ref{lem:combinatorics},
\[
  A_{0}+B_{0}=\{-1,0,1,2\}.
\]
Applying the same lemmas to $-\deg$, whose values on $\mathcal W$ form the same set
$\{-k,-1,0,1,m\}$, gives
\[
  D_{A}+D_{B}=\{-2,-1,0,1\}.
\]
Every $f\in\mathbf X$ is supported in $[\min A_{0},\max D_{A}]$, and every $g\in\mathbf Y$ in
$[\min B_{0},\max D_{B}]$. So every product $fg$ is supported in
\[
  [\min A_{0}+\min B_{0},\ \max D_{A}+\max D_{B}]=[-1,1].
\]
But the product of $f\in\mathbf X$ of order $\max A_{0}$ with $g\in\mathbf Y$ of order $\max B_{0}$
has order $\max(A_{0}+B_{0})=2$ at $0$. This is a contradiction, and Theorem~C is proved.
\qed

\begin{remark}
The proof is two-sided: Lemma~\ref{lem:combinatorics} alone allows $m=2$. The extra
step is that the orders at the two ends of a Laurent polynomial pinch its support. In the
monomial case $P=X^{k}$, the set of orders at $0$ already fails for $k\ge4$, and the proof
reduces to the project's power hulls \cite[Thm.~10.14]{BP}. Theorem~C thus extends the
negative half of that result from monomials to all polynomials. The positive half for
cubics is Diaz's (Section~\ref{sec:statements}).
\end{remark}

\section{Proofs of the corollaries of Theorem C}\label{sec:cor}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:cubic}]
If $P(u)\in\Lt$, then $W_{P(u)}\subseteq\Lt$ (Section~\ref{sec:setting}). By Theorem~C,
$W_{P(u)}$ carries a configuration, which contradicts Theorem~\ref{thm:roy}. Next,
$P(\bar u)=\overline{\bar P(u)}$, where $\bar P$ has conjugated coefficients and the same
degree, and $\Lt$ is stable under conjugation; so $P(\bar u)\notin\Lt$ as well. Finally,
if $e^{\beta P(u)}$ were algebraic, then $\beta P(u)\in\cL\subseteq\Lt$, and so
$P(u)\in\Lt$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:nogo}]
(a) The first claim is Theorem~C(iii). For the second, put $F=\Qbar(u)$, which contains
$V_{0}=W_{P(u)}$, because $\bar u=\rho/u$. The $w_{j}$ are algebraically independent over $F$.
The separation theorem of the project \cite[Thm.~10.16]{BP} puts every $2\times3$
configuration in $V_{0}+\sum_{j}\Qbar w_{j}$ inside $V_{0}$, where there is none.

(b) Since $G$ has real coefficients, $z$ is real. Also
$\Re u=(u+\rho u^{-1})/2$ and $\Im u=(u-\rho u^{-1})/(2i)$, so $z=\ell(u)$ for a Laurent
polynomial $\ell\in\Qbar[T,T^{-1}]$. If $z\in H_{0}$, then $W_{z}=H_{0}$ carries nothing.
Otherwise $W_{z}=H_{0}+\Qbar z$ has dimension $4$. If it carried a configuration,
Theorem~B \cite[Thm.~10.6]{BP} would give $z=h+cw$ with $h\in H_{0}$,
$c\in\Qbar^{\times}$, and $w$ equal to $u^{2}$, $\bar u^{2}$ or $1/(u-a)$ with
$a\in\Qbar^{\times}$. The last is impossible, because $cw=z-h$ would be a Laurent
polynomial in $u$ while $1/(T-a)$ has a pole at $a\neq0$. If $w=u^{2}$, conjugate
$z\equiv cu^{2}$ modulo $H_{0}$; since $z$ is real and $H_{0}$ is conjugation-stable, this
gives $z\equiv\bar c\rho^{2}u^{-2}$. Hence $cu^{2}-\bar c\rho^{2}u^{-2}\in H_{0}$. The monomials
$T^{-2},\dots,T^{2}$ are independent, so $c=0$, a contradiction. The case $w=\bar u^{2}$
is symmetric.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of Theorem E}\label{sec:proofE}

This proof was found in this session three times, independently. The derivations used
different methods: valuations, the ratio $x_{2}/x_{1}$, and computation. They agree on the
statement, and each was checked adversarially with exact computer algebra (sympy). We
give the second in condensed form. It deserves an independent referee before anything is
built on it. Write $W=H\oplus M$ with $M=M_{z}$, and $\mathbf X,\mathbf Y$ for the spans
of a configuration.

\emph{Step 0: reductions.}
(a) By Lemma~\ref{lem:normal}, $\mathbf X,\mathbf Y\subseteq\Qbar[T,T^{-1}]$ with
$\mathbf X\mathbf Y\subseteq W$.
(b) $\dim M\le1$ carries nothing. If $M=0$, argue as for degree at most one in
Theorem~C. If $M=\Qbar m$, Theorem~B puts $T^{2}$, $T^{-2}$ or some $1/(T-a)$ in $W$. The
last is not a Laurent polynomial. The first two force $m\propto T^{\pm2}$, but then
$m^{\sigma}\propto T^{\mp2}\notin\Qbar m$, against $\sigma M=M$. So $\dim M=2$.
(c) Let $O=\ord_{0}(M\setminus0)=\{o_{1}<o_{2}\}$, with $o_{i}\notin\{-1,0,1\}$. Since $\sigma$
exchanges $\ord_{0}$ and $-\deg$, the degrees of $M$ form $-O$. Supports in $H$ and $M$ are
disjoint, so $\ord_{0}$ and $-\deg$ take the same five values $S=\{o_{1},o_{2},-1,0,1\}$ on
$W\setminus0$. If $o_{1}\ge2$, then $M\subseteq T^{2}\Qbar[T]$ and
$\sigma M\subseteq T^{-2}\Qbar[T^{-1}]$, so $M=0$; hence $o_{1}\le-2$. An element of order
$o_{2}$ has degree in $-O$, so $o_{2}\le-o_{1}$.
(d) \emph{Width.} If $o_{2}\le-2$, every $w\in W\setminus H$ has $\ord_{0}w\in O$ and
$\deg w\in-O$.
(e) \emph{The ratio has degree at most two.} Let $h=x_{2}/x_{1}$. We claim
$[\Qbar(T):\Qbar(h)]\le2$. Suppose first the products span four dimensions (Case~I).
Then they form a progression $b,bh,bh^{2},bh^{3}$ \cite[Lemma~4]{BSZ17} (the project's \cite[Lemma~10.2]{BP}). That span meets
$H$ in dimension at least $2$, and the ratio of two independent elements of the
intersection has degree at most $2$ in $T$ and lies in $\Qbar(h)\setminus\Qbar$.
Otherwise the products span all of $W$ (Case~II), and \cite[Thm.~10.58]{BP} applies.
(f) \emph{Pencils.} Write $h=p/q$ with $p,q$ coprime of degree at most $2$. At every
$a\in\Qbar^{\times}$, $p$ or $q$ is non-zero, so replacing $(\mathbf X,\mathbf Y)$ by
$(\spn(p,q),\,(x_{1}/q)\mathbf Y)$ keeps $\mathbf Y$ inside the Laurent polynomials. After
shifting by a power of $T$ and a change of basis, $\spn(p,q)$ is one of the following.
For each we list the orders at $0$ and the values of $-\deg$:
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
T_{1}=\spn(1,T) & \{0,1\} & \{0,-1\}\\
T_{2}=\spn(1,T^{2}) & \{0,2\} & \{0,-2\}\\
T_{3}=\spn(T^{2},T+\beta),\ \beta\neq0 & \{0,2\} & \{-1,-2\}\\
T_{4}=\spn(1,T^{2}+\alpha T),\ \alpha\neq0 & \{0,1\} & \{0,-2\}\\
T_{5a}=\spn(T,T^{2}+\beta),\ \beta\neq0 & \{0,1\} & \{-1,-2\}\\
T_{5b}=\spn(T^{2}+\gamma T,1+\zeta T),\ \gamma\zeta(1-\gamma\zeta)\neq0 & \{0,1\} & \{-1,-2\}
\end{array}
\]
Conjugation maps a configuration in $W$ to one in $W$, and exchanges $T_{3}$ and $T_{4}$.
A ratio of degree one means pencil $T_{1}$.

\emph{Step 1: shapes.} Lemma~\ref{lem:sumset} at $0$ and at $\infty$ requires the gap $d$
of $A_{0}$, and that of $A_{\infty}$, to occur at least three times as a difference in
$S$. This holds at both ends with the same $S$. Counting differences, with
$o_{1}\le-2$ and $o_{2}\le-o_{1}$, leaves the following shapes $O$. The count was checked
by machine for $|o_{1}|\le40$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\{-2,2\}$, $\{-3,2\}$, $\{-3,3\}$, and $\{-3,-2\}$.
\item $\{-k,2\}$ with $k\ge4$; here $d=1$ and $a+B_{0}=\{-1,0,1\}$.
\item $\{o_{1},-2\}$ with $o_{1}\le-5$; here $d=1$ and $a+B_{0}=\{-2,-1,0\}$.
\item $\{c,c+1\}$ with $c\le-4$; here $d=1$ and $a+B_{0}=\{c,-1,0\}$.
\item $\{-4,-2\}$; here $d=1$ with $\{-2,-1,0\}$, or $d=2$ with $\{-4,-2,-1\}$.
\item $\{-5,-3\}$; here $d=2$.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Step 2: the two-sided pinch.} For $\{-k,2\}$ the argument of Theorem~C applies.
Products are supported in $[-1,1]$, yet one has order $2$. Now take $\{o_{1},-2\}$, or
$\{-4,-2\}$ with $d=1$ at both ends. Products are supported in $[-2,2]$, so they lie in
$H\oplus(M\cap\spn(T^{-2},T^{2}))$, and they span at least four dimensions. That
intersection is then a conjugation-stable line, $\Qbar(T^{-2}+eT^{2})$ with $e\neq0$. The
products therefore form a configuration in a four-dimensional extension of $H$ that
contains neither $T^{2}$ nor $T^{-2}$, against Theorem~B.

\emph{Step 3: widths.} For $\{-5,-3\}$, gap $2$ at both ends forces $T_{2}$. Then
$\mathbf Y\subseteq W$ and $T^{2}\mathbf Y\subseteq W$, so every $y\in\mathbf Y$ has degree at most
$5-2=3$. Its $M$-part therefore lies in the line of elements of $M$ of degree at most
$3$. Moreover $\mathbf Y\cap H$ has orders in $B_{0}\cap\{-1,0,1\}=\{-1\}$, so it has
dimension at most one, and $\dim\mathbf Y\le2$. For $\{-4,-2\}$ with $d=2$ at $0$ (the case
at $\infty$ is the conjugate), the pencil is $T_{2}$ or $T_{3}$. For $T_{2}$ the same
count applies. For $T_{3}$, $B_{0}=\{-4,-2,-1\}$ and the end at $\infty$ forces
$\deg y\le0$ on $\mathbf Y$. Then the element $y$ of order $-4$ gives $(T+\beta)y\in W$ of
order $-4$ and degree at most $1$, against the width property.

\emph{Step 4: a ratio of degree one gives (R).} Let $V_{1}=W\cap T^{-1}W\supseteq\mathbf Y$.
Then $\sigma V_{1}=W\cap TW=TV_{1}$. If $\dim V_{1}\ge4$, its orders form an interval
$B$ with $B+\{0,1\}\subseteq S$, so $S=[-2,2]$ and $O=\{-2,2\}$, which is (P). The other
possibility, $[-3,1]$, puts $T^{2}$ in $M$, which is absurd. So $V_{1}=\spn(T^{-1},1,g)$
and $W=\spn(T^{-1},1,T,g,Tg)$; otherwise Case~I contains $H$, and Theorem~B gives (P)
again. From $\sigma V_{1}=TV_{1}$ we get $g^{\sigma}=\alpha+\beta T+\gamma Tg$. Applying
$\sigma$ once more gives $|\gamma|=1$ and $\alpha=-\gamma\bar\beta$. Then
$g'=g-\bar\beta T^{-1}$ satisfies $g'^{\sigma}=\gamma Tg'$. Comparing coefficients gives
$g'\equiv G+\bar\gamma T^{-1}G^{\sigma}$ modulo $\spn(1,T^{-1})$, with $G\in T\Qbar[T]$. If
$\deg G=1$, then $T^{2}\in M$, which is (P). Otherwise we are in (R), with $\kappa=\bar\gamma$.

\emph{Step 5: the shape $\{c,c+1\}$ with $c\le-4$ is (R).} Gap $1$ at both ends forces
$T_{1}$ or $T_{5}$. Take $T_{5}$. Then $B_{0}=\{c,-1,0\}$, and the degrees on $\mathbf Y$ are
$\{-c-2,-1,-2\}$. The subspace of $\mathbf Y$ of degree at most $-1$ is two-dimensional,
so it contains some $y_{1}$ of order $c$. With $p_{0}\in\mathbf X$, $p_{0}(0)\neq0$, the
product $p_{0}y_{1}\in W$ has order $c$ and degree at most $1$, against the width property.
So the pencil is $T_{1}$, and Step~4 applies.

\emph{Step 6: the shape $\{-3,-2\}$.} Here $M\subseteq\spn(T^{\pm2},T^{\pm3})$ and
$M\cap\spn(T^{2},T^{3})=0$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $T_{2}$, $T_{4}$, with $h=T^{2}+\alpha T$. Any $w=h_{0}+m$ with $m\neq0$ in $M$
has $\deg hw\ge4$, which is outside $W$. So $\mathbf Y\subseteq H$, and
$h\cdot\mathbf Y\subseteq W$ then forces $\mathbf Y\subseteq\Qbar T^{-1}$. The case $T_{3}$ is the
conjugate.
\item $T_{5b}$. Then $\mathbf Y$ is supported in $[-3,1]$. Write $n_{2}$ for the element of
$M$ of degree $2$ with leading coefficient $1$, and $n_{-2}$ for the element of order
$-2$ with lowest coefficient $1$. Then $(1+\zeta T)y\in H+\Qbar n_{2}$ and
$(T^{2}+\gamma T)y\in H+\Qbar n_{-2}$. This gives six linear equations in seven unknowns.
We checked by a Gr\"obner basis that the coefficient matrix has rank at least $5$ for
every admissible choice of parameters. So $\dim\mathbf Y\le2$.
\item $T_{5a}$, with $h=T+\beta T^{-1}$. For $hw$ to stay in $W$, the $M$-part of each
$w\in\mathbf Y$ must lie in $M_{0}=M\cap\spn(T^{-2},T^{2})$. If $M_{0}=0$, then
$\dim\mathbf Y\le1$. Otherwise $M_{0}=\Qbar(T^{-2}+eT^{2})$ with $|e|=1$. Modulo $H$,
$h\bigl(xT^{-1}+y+zT+t(T^{-2}+eT^{2})\bigr)\equiv\beta xT^{-2}+zT^{2}+t(\beta T^{-3}+eT^{3})$.
So $\dim\mathbf Y\ge3$ if and only if
$M\subseteq\spn(T^{-2},T^{2},\beta T^{-3}+eT^{3})$. Conjugation-stability then gives
$|\beta|=1$ and $\zeta=-\beta e^{2}\bar\zeta$: this is (Q).
\item $T_{1}$ gives (R), by Step~4.
\end{itemize}
Conversely, let $M\subseteq\spn(T^{\pm2},T^{\pm3})$ be conjugation-stable with
$\dim(M\cap\spn(T^{\pm2}))=1$. Then the $T^{\pm3}$-coefficients $a,d$ of a second generator
satisfy $|a|=|d|$, and $M$ normalises to the form in (Q).

\emph{Step 7: the shapes $\{-2,2\}$, $\{-3,2\}$, $\{-3,3\}$ are (P).} An element of $M$ of
order at least $2$ has degree at most $-o_{1}\le3$. So it lies in $\spn(T^{2},T^{3})$, and
Theorem~C gives the configurations.

\emph{Configurations, and disjointness.} The configurations listed in (Q) and (R) were
checked symbolically for general parameters: all six products lie in $W$, and both
families are free. In (R), the identity $Tg=\kappa g^{\sigma}$ is a direct computation. On
disjointness: (Q) has $M\cap\spn(T^{2},T^{3})=0$, so it is not (P). Its intersection
with $\spn(T^{\pm2})$ is non-zero, while in (R) that intersection is $0$ (computed from
$G$), so it is not (R). And (R) with $\deg G\ge2$ has shape $\{-\deg G-1,-\deg G\}$, which
is not a (P) shape. \qed

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:E}]
(a) An exponent of absolute value at least $4$ in $\pi L$ gives $o_{1}\le-4$, because
$M$ is conjugation-stable. Of the shapes in Step~1, only $\{c,c+1\}$ with $c\le-4$
survives Steps~2 and~3, and it is (R).
(b) In terms of $T$, let the coefficients of $\pi L$ at $T^{-3},T^{-2},T^{2},T^{3}$ be
$a=As^{3}$, $b=Bs^{2}$, $c=Cs^{2}$ and $d=Ds^{3}$. Those of $\pi L^{\sigma}$ are
$\bar d,\bar c,\bar b,\bar a$. A combination lies in $\spn(T^{2},T^{3})$ iff it kills
$T^{-3}$ and $T^{-2}$, which needs $\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\ \bar d&\bar c\end{pmatrix}=0$,
that is $\Delta=0$. A combination lies in $\spn(T^{\pm2})$ iff it kills $T^{\pm3}$, which
needs $|a|^{2}-|d|^{2}=0$, that is $N=0$. For (R) the condition is $|\Delta|=s|N|\neq0$.
This was derived by one of the three derivations and re-derived by its referee; it
checks against $g=G+\kappa T^{-1}G^{\sigma}$ with $\deg G=2$, where $N=|g_{2}|^{2}/s^{6}$ and
$|\Delta|=|g_{2}|^{2}/s^{5}$.
\end{proof}
\section{Proof of Theorem D}\label{sec:proofD}

\begin{lemma}[Adjoining a generic point of a circle]\label{lem:inversion}
Let $F\subseteq\C$ be a field containing $\Qbar$, let $V_{0}\subseteq F$ be a $\Qbar$-subspace,
and let $u$ be transcendental over $F$. Then every configuration in
$V_{0}+\Qbar u+\Qbar u^{-1}$ has all six products in $V_{0}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
As in Lemma~\ref{lem:normal}, we may assume $x_{i},y_{j}\in F(u)$; identify $F(u)$ with
$F(T)$. Then $Tx_{i}y_{j}=P_{ij}(T)=m_{ij}+c_{ij}T+l_{ij}T^{2}$ with $m_{ij},l_{ij}\in\Qbar$
and $c_{ij}\in V_{0}$. The matrix $(P_{ij})$ has rank one over $F(T)$. Since $F[T]$ is a
principal ideal domain, $P_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}$ with $a\in F[T]^{2}$ \emph{primitive}, meaning
$\gcd(a_{1},a_{2})=1$, and $b\in F[T]^{3}$, all entries non-zero. Take $a$ proportional to a
non-zero column and divide by the gcd; every column is then $a$ times an element of
$F(T)$, and that element is a polynomial because $\gcd(a_{1},a_{2})=1$.
Then $x_{i}=\kappa a_{i}$ and $y_{j}=\kappa'b_{j}$ for some $\kappa,\kappa'\in F(T)^{\times}$
independent of $i$ and $j$. So the $a_{i}$ are linearly independent over $\Qbar$, and so
are the $b_{j}$. Write
$a=\sum_{r}\alpha_{r}T^{r}$ and $b=\sum_{r}\beta_{r}T^{r}$ with $\alpha_{r}\in F^{2}$ and
$\beta_{r}\in F^{3}$, and let $\delta_{a},\delta_{b}$ be the largest degrees of the
entries. Then $\delta_{a}+\delta_{b}\le2$. The constant coefficients give
$\alpha_{0}\beta_{0}^{\mathsf T}=(m_{ij})$, with entries in $\Qbar$. If
$\delta_{a}+\delta_{b}=2$, the coefficients of $T^{2}$ give
$\alpha_{\delta_{a}}\beta_{\delta_{b}}^{\mathsf T}=(l_{ij})$, also with entries in $\Qbar$.

We use one observation repeatedly. If $\gamma\delta^{\mathsf T}$ is non-zero with entries
in $\Qbar$, then $\gamma$ and $\delta$ are each an element of $F$ times a vector with
algebraic entries. A vector $b$ of that form has $\Qbar$-dependent entries, and a vector
$a$ of that form has $a_{2}/a_{1}\in\Qbar$.

\emph{Case $\delta_{a}=0$.} Then $a\in F^{2}$, with both entries non-zero. If
$\beta_{0}\neq0$, then $a\beta_{0}^{\mathsf T}\neq0$ has algebraic entries, so
$a_{2}/a_{1}\in\Qbar$, which is excluded. Hence $\beta_{0}=0$, and likewise $\beta_{2}=0$.
So $b_{j}=\beta_{j1}T$, and $x_{i}y_{j}=a_{i}\beta_{j1}=c_{ij}\in V_{0}$.

\emph{Case $\delta_{a}\ge1$.} If $\alpha_{0}=0$, then $T$ divides $a$, against
primitivity. So $\alpha_{0}\neq0$. If $\delta_{b}=0$, then $b=\beta_{0}$ and
$\alpha_{0}\beta_{0}^{\mathsf T}\neq0$, so $b$ has dependent entries. Otherwise
$\delta_{a}=\delta_{b}=1$, and both $\alpha_{0}\beta_{0}^{\mathsf T}$ (when $\beta_{0}\neq0$)
and $\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}^{\mathsf T}\neq0$ have algebraic entries. Then every $b_{j}$ lies
in $\Qbar f_{0}+\Qbar f_{1}T$ for some $f_{0},f_{1}\in F$, a space of dimension at most two.
So the three $b_{j}$ are dependent. Every case with $\delta_{a}\ge1$ is contradictory.
\end{proof}

Take $F=\Qbar(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})$ and $V_{0}=\Qbar+\sum_{j}\Qbar w_{j}$, with
$u,w_{1},\dots,w_{m}$ algebraically independent. Together with the separation theorem
\cite[Thm.~10.16]{BP}, which leaves no configuration in this $V_{0}$, the lemma then
gives Theorem~A of the project \cite[Thm.~10.1]{BP}. The method is that of the project's
proof of Theorem~A: factor a rank-one polynomial matrix and compare degrees. The
separation theorem allows any $V_{0}\subseteq F$, but it needs the adjoined numbers to be
algebraically independent over $F$, and $u,u^{-1}$ are not. The lemma does both at once.

\begin{lemma}[Subsets of transcendence degree one]\label{lem:trdeg}
In the setting of Theorem~D, let $V\subseteq\cL_{u}$ be a finite-dimensional $\Q$-subspace
with $\trdeg_{\Q}\Q(V)\le1$. Then $V\subseteq\cL$, or $V\subseteq\Q u+\Q\bar u$, or
$\dim_{\Q}V\le1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The sum $\cL_{u}=\cL\oplus\Q u\oplus\Q\bar u$ is direct, since $\lambda+pu+q\rho u^{-1}=0$ with
$\lambda\in F$ forces $p=q=0$, $u$ being transcendental over $F$. Let
$\pi\colon\cL_{u}\to\Q u\oplus\Q\bar u$ be the projection. Two facts are used throughout. A number
$\lambda+pu+q\bar u$ with $(p,q)\neq(0,0)$ is transcendental over $F$. And an element of
$F$ that is algebraic over $\Q(e)$, for some $e$ transcendental over $F$, is algebraic,
hence $0$ if it lies in $\cL$.

\emph{If $\dim\pi(V)=2$}, pick $e_{1}=\lambda_{1}+u$ and $e_{2}=\lambda_{2}+\bar u$ in $V$.
They are algebraically dependent, say $\Phi(e_{1},e_{2})=0$ with
$\Phi\in\Q[X,Y]\setminus\{0\}$. As $u$ is transcendental over $F$, $\Phi$ vanishes on the
hyperbola $(X-\lambda_{1})(Y-\lambda_{2})=\rho$: the element
$\Phi(\lambda_{1}+T,\lambda_{2}+\rho/T)$ of $F(T)$ vanishes at $T=u$, hence is zero. So the
irreducible polynomial $\Psi=(X-\lambda_{1})(Y-\lambda_{2})-\rho$ divides $\Phi$ in
$\C[X,Y]$. Irreducible factors over $\Qbar$ stay irreducible over $\C$, so $\Psi$ is a scalar
multiple of a polynomial over $\Qbar$. Its coefficient of $XY$ is $1$, so $\Psi$ itself has
algebraic coefficients. Hence
$\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\cL\cap\Qbar=\{0\}$. Then any $e=\lambda+pu+q\bar u\in V$ gives
$\lambda=e-pe_{1}-qe_{2}\in V$. This $\lambda$ is algebraic over $\Q(V)$, so over $\Q(u)$,
and therefore $\lambda=0$. Thus $V\subseteq\Q u+\Q\bar u$.

\emph{If $\dim\pi(V)=1$}, pick $e_{0}\in V$ with $\pi(e_{0})\neq0$; it is transcendental
over $F$. Every $\lambda\in V\cap\cL$ is algebraic over $\Q(e_{0})$, so $\lambda=0$. Hence
$V\cap\cL=0$, and $\dim V=\dim\pi(V)=1$.

\emph{If $\pi(V)=0$}, then $V\subseteq\cL$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~D]
(D1) Conjugation maps $\cL+\Q u+\Q\bar u$ onto $\cL+\Q\bar u+\Q u$.

(D2) Let $\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\cL_{u}$ be independent over $\Q$, and suppose
$\beta_{0}+\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}=0$ with $\beta_{i}\in\Qbar$. Changing the $\Q$-basis of
$\spn_{\Q}(\lambda_{i})$, we may assume $\lambda_{r+1},\dots,\lambda_{n}$ span the kernel of
$\pi$, which is $\spn\cap\cL$, and that $\pi(\lambda_{1}),\dots,\pi(\lambda_{r})$ are
independent, with $r\le2$. Since $1,u,u^{-1}$ are independent over $F$, the coefficients
of $u$ and $\bar u$ in the relation vanish: $\sum_{i\le r}\beta_{i}\pi(\lambda_{i})=0$. Vectors
of $\Q^{2}$ independent over $\Q$ remain so over $\Qbar$, so $\beta_{i}=0$ for $i\le r$.
What remains is $\beta_{0}+\sum_{i>r}\beta_{i}\lambda_{i}=0$, with $\Q$-independent
$\lambda_{i}\in\cL$, and Baker's theorem \cite{Bak66} gives $\beta=0$. Hermite--Lindemann
is the case $n=1$. For Gel'fond--Schneider: if $\beta\notin\Q$, then $\lambda$ and
$\beta\lambda$ are $\Q$-independent, yet $\beta\cdot\lambda-1\cdot\beta\lambda=0$.

(D3) Take $V_{0}=\Lt\subseteq F$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion}, with $\bar u=\rho u^{-1}$. A
configuration in $\Lt_{u}$ would be one in $\Lt$, against Theorem~\ref{thm:roy}. Now let
$x$ and $y$ be $\Q$-independent with all products in $\cL_{u}$. If $x_{2}=\beta x_{1}$ with
$\beta\in\Qbar$, then $\beta\notin\Q$ and $x_{1}y_{1},\beta x_{1}y_{1}\in\cL_{u}$, against
(D2). If $\sum_{j}\beta_{j}y_{j}=0$ non-trivially, then the $x_{1}y_{j}\in\cL_{u}$ are
$\Qbar$-dependent. By (D2) they are $\Q$-dependent, so $y$ is $\Q$-dependent. Hence
$x$ and $y$ are $\Qbar$-independent, and $x\otimes y$ is a configuration in $\Lt_{u}$,
against the first part.

(D4) Let $V$ be the $\Q$-span of the entries of $N$. If $V\subseteq\cL$, this is the four
exponentials theorem in transcendence degree one, due to Waldschmidt and Brownawell
\cite[Thm.~6.31]{BP}. If $\dim V=1$, the entries are $r_{ij}e$ with $r_{ij}\in\Q$, so
$\det(r_{ij})=0$ and the rows are $\Q$-dependent. If $V\subseteq\Q u+\Q\bar u$, the entries
are rational linear forms in $(u,\bar u)$. The determinant is a rational quadratic form
$aX^{2}+bXY+cY^{2}$ vanishing at $(u,\rho/u)$. Multiplying by $u^{2}$ gives
$au^{4}+b\rho u^{2}+c\rho^{2}=0$, so $a=b=c=0$. A $2\times2$ matrix of linear forms whose
determinant vanishes identically has dependent rows or columns \cite[Lemma~12.1]{BP}.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:trdeg} these cases are exhaustive.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Scope of Theorem D]\label{rem:scopeD}
The system $(\cL_{u},\Lt_{u})$ has no exponential function. It models statements about
membership in $\cL$ and $\Lt$, not statements about the values $e^{x_{i}y_{j}}$. One such
statement is Waldschmidt's theorem of 1973 \cite{W73}, which the project uses in its
``mixed rigidity'' \cite[Thm.~7.11]{BP}. In that form, $x_{1},x_{2}$ and $y_{1},y_{2}$ are
$\Q$-linearly independent, and $e^{x_{1}y_{2}},e^{x_{2}y_{2}}$ are algebraic. Then two of the
eight numbers $x_{i},y_{j},e^{x_{i}y_{j}}$ are algebraically independent. A proof of Diaz's
conjecture through such a statement is not ruled out here.

Nor is it known whether Roy's rank bound for larger matrices holds in
$(\cL_{u},\Lt_{u})$. By \cite[Cor.~1]{Roy92} (p.~38; also quoted in \cite{Mak23}), a matrix with
entries in $\Lt$ has rank at least $m\theta/(1+\theta)$, where
$\theta$ is computed from its zero blocks after changes of basis. This is a lower bound,
not an exact rank. If the bound fails in the model, it can see a generic
candidate. We probed it at the first open case, $u^{4}\in\Lt$
(Section~\ref{sec:meaning}).
\end{remark}


\section{What this means for Diaz's conjecture}\label{sec:meaning}

\paragraph{The reach of Roy's theorem through one hull.}
Theorems~C and~E answer, for every $z$ that is a polynomial in $u$ and $\bar u$, whether
Roy's strong six exponentials theorem can refute ``$z\in\Lt$'' at a candidate using only
$z$, its conjugate and the certificate $H_{0}$. Every exclusion they list is a routine
substitution, into Diaz's theorems or into Roy's. What was not found in the sources read is the converse. Apart
from classes (P), (Q) and (R) nothing can be excluded this way. For a polynomial in $u$
alone the limit is degree three, a precise form, for one polynomial in $u$, of Diaz's remark that
within the strong six exponentials theorem one cannot hope to go far \cite[p.~390]{Dia07}. This concerns \emph{one}
hypothetical logarithm at a time. Two ties together can reach further (for example
$u^{4}$ and $u^{5}$; Corollary~\ref{cor:nogo}). So can additional numbers that are
algebraically dependent on $u$.

\paragraph{Why every known route is conditional.}
The project derives Diaz's conjecture from several principles: the strong four
exponentials conjecture \cite[Conj.~11.17]{Wal00}, Waldschmidt's strong five exponentials
conjecture, and the structural rank conjecture. Each lets algebraic constants enter the
products, and each fails in $(\cL_{u},\Lt_{u})$, because the certificate
$(1,u)\otimes(1,\bar u)$ violates it. The theorems in (D2)--(D4) hold there (Theorem~D).
So a proof must use some property of $(\cL,\Lt)$, or of the exponential function, that
fails in $(\cL_{u},\Lt_{u})$. Three kinds are not excluded:
\begin{itemize}
\item an inhomogeneous principle of the kind above;
\item a statement about the values $e^{x_{i}y_{j}}$, such as Waldschmidt's theorem of
1973, or Roy's rank bound for larger matrices if it fails in the model
(Remark~\ref{rem:scopeD});
\item the way a genuine candidate, which lies in $\cL$ and so is not generic over $F$,
is tied to the other logarithms.
\end{itemize}
Under Kirby's weak Schanuel conjecture, candidates satisfy $\Im u\in\pi\Q$
\cite[Prop.~13.20]{BP}. So $u$ is algebraic over $\Q(\pi)\subseteq F$: such candidates are
never generic, and Theorem~D says nothing about them. On that branch the
four exponentials theorem in transcendence degree one does give unconditional results;
those are the project's Chapter~13. The tie to $i\pi$ lies inside $H_{0}$, so it gives
Roy's theorem nothing further through hulls.

\paragraph{Higher rank: a first probe.}
Rank-one configurations cannot exclude $u^{4}$ (the power hulls). In this session we
asked whether Roy's rank bound for larger matrices can, at rank two. The space is
$V=\spn(u^{-4},u^{-1},1,u,u^{4})$. The question reduces by a torus argument to graded
monomial matrices, then to a combinatorial ``colour'' model of rank-two matrices with
proved bounds $d,l\le10$. Searched exhaustively by a SAT solver, and independently by a
second enumeration up to $d,l\le13$ that assumes no window, the model has no solution.
So \emph{at rank two, Roy's bound does not exclude $u^{4}$}. The same holds for the hull of
$u^{4}+cu^{3}$. Controls behave as they should: the method finds the known configurations
in $\spn(u^{-2},\dots,u^{2})$. Rank three and above is open. For the certificate space alone, the
bound gives nothing at any rank. This is Roy's lemma on spaces of dimension at most
three \cite[Th.~3.4]{Roy95} (also \cite[Exercise~12.10]{Wal00}): rank $\ge(d+l)/4\ge dl/(d+l)$.
These results are computer-assisted, use Roy's bound in the quoted form, and are not part
of the theorems above.

\paragraph{Next steps.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)}]
\item Settle whether class (Q) is in the project's unformalised classification
(Remark~\ref{rem:Qnew}), and formalise Theorems~C(iii) and~E. Their inputs, Theorem~B, the
progression lemma and the degree bound, are already formal.
\item Extend Theorem~E from Laurent polynomials to all of $\Qbar(u)$. Poles off
$\{0,\infty\}$ come in conjugate pairs $a$, $\rho/\bar a$, and the order and degree sets
become one sumset condition per place.
\item Roy's rank bound at rank three for $u^{4}$, and whether the bound holds in the
generic model $(\cL_{u},\Lt_{u})$.
\end{enumerate}
